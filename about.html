Proposed academic initiative — September 2028

About the Initiative

Building an environment in which disagreement becomes an opportunity for deeper understanding.

Why This Initiative?

Students are often asked to hold an opinion long before they are taught how to test one. This workshop reverses that order.

  1. Global problems rarely have simple answers

    Questions in economics and public policy involve trade-offs between real interests. Treating them as settled teaches students the wrong lesson about what knowledge is.

  2. Understanding the opposing argument is a learned skill

    A student should be able to state a position they disagree with so accurately that its own supporters would call the account fair. That ability requires deliberate practice.

  3. Academic debate must rest on evidence

    Rhetoric without evidence produces confident students and fragile conclusions. Every claim in this workshop must be traceable to something that can be checked.

  4. International students bring different vantage points

    Participants come from different countries and education systems. What one group treats as obvious is often the first thing another group questions.

  5. Writing turns discussion into reasoning

    Debate can reward quick thinking; an article does not. Writing forces a student to show the structure of the argument, document the sources, and face its weakest point.

The Academic Model

One question moving through eight stages over a seven-day program, from independent research to two completed drafts.

  2. 02

  3. 03

  4. 04

    Independent research

  5. 05

    Academic mentorship

  6. 06

    Structured debate

  7. 07

    Collaborative writing

  8. 08

    Two final articles

The Two-Sided Principle

No team is asked to "win". Positions are assigned rather than chosen, and success is measured by the quality of the reasoning rather than by an audience verdict.

By the end of the program, each group should be able to answer five questions about its own position and about the position it argued against:

  • Why the argument is persuasive
  • Where its weaknesses lie
  • What evidence supports it
  • What evidence counts against it
  • Which assumptions it rests on

A group that defends its own side well but cannot describe the other side fairly has not completed the exercise.

Academic Mentors

The initiative proposes to involve at least four professors or academic researchers, working with both groups rather than with one side. No invitations have been issued yet, and every participation depends on availability and institutional approval.

Questioning assumptions

Asking students what their argument requires in order to be true, and what it quietly takes for granted.

Guiding research

Pointing students to the relevant literature and helping them judge the quality of what they find.

Raising the quality of argument

Tightening the structure of a position and removing reasoning that does not survive scrutiny.

Holding students to the evidence

Tying every claim to a documented source, and distinguishing correlation from causal explanation.

Reviewing drafts

Reading both articles as they develop and commenting on clarity, method, and documentation.

Giving academic feedback

Giving students a realistic sense of the standard expected in university-level work.

Expected Outcomes

What participants should be better able to do after the workshop than before it.

Research skills

Finding primary sources and data, and documenting them properly.

Critical thinking

Testing the structure of an argument instead of reacting to its conclusion.

Policy analysis

Reading a policy question in terms of trade-offs, costs, and constraints.

Academic writing

Producing structured, documented prose built on a defensible thesis.

Cross-cultural communication

Working productively with peers who frame the same problem differently.

Intellectual humility

Recognising the limits of the available evidence, and stating them in writing.
