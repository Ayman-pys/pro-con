Proposed academic initiative — September 2028

About the Initiative

Building an environment where disagreement becomes an opportunity for deeper understanding.

Why This Initiative?

Students are often asked to hold an opinion long before they are taught how to test one. This workshop inverts that order.

  • Global problems rarely have simple answers

    Questions in economics and public policy involve trade-offs between real interests. Treating them as solved questions teaches students the wrong lesson about how knowledge works.

  • Understanding an opposing argument is a skill

    Students should be able to state a position they disagree with so accurately that its own defenders would recognise it. That ability has to be practised deliberately.

  • Academic debate must be evidence-based

    Rhetorical skill without evidence produces confident students and weak conclusions. Every claim made in this workshop must be traceable to something that can be checked.

  • International students bring different perspectives

    Participants arrive from different countries and educational systems. The assumptions one group treats as self-evident are often exactly what another group questions first.

  • Writing turns discussion into reasoning

    A debate can reward speed. An article cannot. Writing forces students to make their structure explicit, cite their sources, and confront the weakest part of their own case.

The Academic Model

One question moves through eight stages in a single day, from independent research to two finished drafts.

  1. 1

    25 students

  2. 2

    One central question

  3. 3

    Two opposing teams

  4. 4

    Independent research

  5. 5

    Academic mentorship

  6. 6

    Structured debate

  7. 7

    Collaborative writing

  8. 8

    Two final articles

The Two-Sided Principle

Neither team is assigned to win. Positions are allocated, not chosen, and success is measured by the quality of the reasoning rather than the verdict of an audience.

By the end of the day, each group should be able to answer five questions about the position it was given, and about the position it opposed:

  • Why the argument is persuasive
  • Where its weaknesses are
  • What evidence supports it
  • What evidence challenges it
  • What assumptions it depends upon

A group that argues its side well but cannot describe the other side fairly has not completed the exercise.

Academic Mentors

The initiative proposes to involve at least four professors or academic researchers. Mentors would work with both groups rather than represent a side. Invitations have not yet been issued, and all participation is subject to availability and institutional approval.

Challenge assumptions

Ask students what their argument would require to be true, and what it quietly takes for granted.

Guide research

Point students toward relevant literature and help them judge the quality of what they find.

Improve argument quality

Tighten the structure of a case and remove reasoning that does not survive scrutiny.

Encourage evidence

Hold each claim to a documented source, and distinguish correlation from explanation.

Review drafts

Read the developing articles and comment on clarity, method, and use of citations.

Provide academic feedback

Give students a realistic sense of the standard expected in university-level work.

Expected Outcomes

What participants should be able to do more confidently after the workshop than before it.

Research skills

Locating primary sources and data, and citing them properly.

Critical thinking

Testing an argument's structure instead of reacting to its conclusion.

Public-policy analysis

Reading a policy question in terms of trade-offs, costs, and constraints.

Academic writing

Producing structured, referenced prose with a defensible thesis.

Cross-cultural communication

Working productively with peers who frame the same problem differently.

Intellectual humility

Recognising the limits of one's own evidence, and saying so in writing.
